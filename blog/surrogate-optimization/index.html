Surrogate optimization, and when the surface pays for itself

Fit a cheap model to the evaluations you have already paid for, then spend the next one at that model's minimum. The idea is one paragraph long. The arithmetic that decides whether you can afford it is the part people skip.

Mehdi Ghasemi · 27 November 2026 · methods

TL;DR

  • A surrogate replaces try something, measure it with model what you have measured, then choose. You stop paying for directions the surface already says are bad.
  • The warm-up does not scale with your search. min_evals defaults to a flat 25, while a full cubic surface in n parameters has C(n+3, n) terms: 20 at three parameters, 56 at five, 165 at eight. At the defaults, a thirty-evaluation run hands the surface five of its thirty evaluations.
  • On the six-dataset equal-budget suite this search is at parity with Optuna-TPE at 30 evaluations. At 60 the better surface matches or beats every other arm on all six datasets — but the 60-evaluation extension never ran TPE, so that gap is unmeasured rather than won. On a 1,220-pipeline space the search reaches within 1 % of its own final best in a median of 6 evaluations. If one evaluation costs seconds rather than minutes, none of that matters — use TPE.

What the loop does

Hyperparameter optimization is expensive for a boring reason: every candidate you try costs a full cross-validation. Grid search and random search pay that cost at every attempt and keep nothing between attempts. The thousandth candidate is chosen exactly as blindly as the first.

A surrogate changes what you keep. After a warm-up of random evaluations you fit a regression model to the pairs (parameters, score), and that model becomes a stand-in for the expensive function. Minimizing the stand-in is cheap, so you can do it thousands of times per second and see where it wants you to go. You then evaluate the real objective at that one point, add the result, refit, and go again.

In SKSurrogate the whole loop is a BaseSearchCV subclass, so the call shape is the one you already know:

from SKSurrogate import SurrogateRandomCV

search = SurrogateRandomCV(
    estimator,
    param_distributions={
        'C': (1e-3, 10.0),                    # numeric range: a tuple
        'kernel': ['poly', 'rbf'],            # categorical: a list
    },
    n_iter=30,          # candidates actually evaluated with cross-validation
    min_evals=25,       # random evaluations before the sampler hands over
    random_state=0,
).fit(X, y)

Four steps, in the order the code runs them.

  1. Sample. Draw a point from the feasible set. For the first min_evals evaluations this is random; this is the only part of the budget that is not chosen. Three samplers ship: a point in the whole feasible set, a cube around the incumbent, and a sphere around it.
  2. Fit. Once there are enough points, fit the response surface. The default is a stock, un-tuned RBF GaussianProcessRegressor; any scikit-learn regressor can be passed as regressor=.
  3. Minimize. Find the minimum of the surface with scipy.optimize.minimize. This is where the search decides what to do next, and where constraints and the trust region are applied.
  4. Evaluate and update. Score the real objective at the proposed point, add it to the set, refit. Repeat until the budget is spent.

The warm-up is dimension-blind

Whether any of this reaches you is decided before the first model is fitted. The code sizes its warm-up from the surface it was built around: a full cubic in n variables has C(n + 3, n) coefficients, so that is the count it treats as "enough points to fit".

min_evals, the knob that actually controls the handover, does not vary with your search. It defaults to 25. So the default is generous for a three-parameter space (25 points for a 20-term surface) and short from four parameters on (25 points for 35):

I measured what that does at the defaults instead of quoting the table. A three-parameter search with a thirty-evaluation budget finishes with twenty-five samples and five surrogate proposals — the surface gets one evaluation in six. Under twenty-five evaluations it gets none at all: a ten-evaluation run is a random search that writes a longer log.

Now read the benchmark table with that in mind. The B = 30 suite engages its surface at the twentieth record, so two-thirds of the budget is spent sampling before a single proposal exists. Parity with Optuna-TPE at B = 30 is not a disappointment, it is the arithmetic. That twentieth record is worth knowing about too: the runner asked for six evaluations and the constructor of the day raised it to 20, because a cubic in three variables has 20 terms. Since 0.8.1 an explicit min_evals is honoured verbatim and only the default is floored, so the same request today would fire the surface after six. "Surrogate optimization didn't help on my three-knob grid search" is, by contrast, a true statement that says nothing about the method.

At B = 60 the surface finally has a window, and SRS with the Gaussian-process surface matches or beats every other arm on all six datasets. That extension did not include a TPE arm, so "the surrogate beats TPE at sixty evaluations" is a sentence the published data does not support and this post does not claim it.

Two things sampling cannot do

Two mechanisms in the loop are worth knowing about because neither has a random-search equivalent.

A trust region that contracts. With search_sphere=True the proposal is confined to a ball around the incumbent, and the radius shrinks by a factor you set (0.95 by default on SurrogateRandomCV) for every two failed local optimizations. In a high-dimensional space this is what stops the search from spending expensive evaluations on distant, flat regions once it has found something worth refining. Repeated sampling inside a box or sphere tightens the same way.

Constraints the solver actually honours. You can pass ineq=[...], a list of callables whose positivity region is the feasible set. These are handed to SciPy as real inequality constraints, not as penalty terms or rejection sampling. The detail I care about is the guard around it. SciPy's optimize.minimize accepts a constraints= argument for L-BFGS-B, TNC, BFGS, Powell and a few others and then ignores it after emitting a warning, so the point it returns need not be feasible at all. SKSurrogate refuses that combination at construction time:

ValueError: SurrogateRandomCV(scipy_solver=...)('L-BFGS-B') cannot handle inequality
constraints: scipy.optimize.minimize accepts the constraints argument for this method
and then ignores it, so the returned point need not be feasible.
Use one of COBYLA, COBYQA, SLSQP, trust-constr, or remove the constraints.

Four solvers honour nonlinear constraints; SLSQP is the default. A silent infeasible answer is worse than an error, and this is the cheapest possible place to catch it.

What the surface is allowed to be

Six named surfaces ship with the package: a Gaussian process (response-normalized, over a Matérn × white-noise kernel grid), kernel ridge, support-vector regression, a GP with an ARD kernel sized to the data, a degree-3 polynomial ridge, and Bayesian ridge for the cheapest smooth linear-Gaussian fit. All six are smooth point predictors, and that is not a stylistic preference. The local step hands the surface to scipy.optimize.minimize, which differentiates by finite differences, so a piecewise-constant surface — a random forest, a gradient-boosted ensemble — would stall the subproblem it is supposed to be guiding.

This matters because a superficially reasonable idea is therefore excluded. The regime study behind the benchmark runs found that a random forest is the robust generalist across objective families, winning on rational and trigonometric shapes where the smooth surfaces lose. That is a statement about surfaces as predictors. It is not a licence to plug a random forest into this loop, where it will not move. In SKSurrogate, tree ensembles are available for candidate-pool ranking at design time and are deliberately kept out of the surrogate loop.

The receipts

Every search can hand back its own record, and the record is JSON-safe without a custom encoder: json.dumps(search.to_audit_dict()). The bundle carries the estimator and its parameters, the search space as it was actually encoded, the scoring and CV summary, the budget, the termination state, one record per evaluated candidate carrying a zero-based trial index, a surrogate block with fit and failure counters, and a separate list recording how each proposal originated (init, explore, surrogate_exploit).

The encoded search space is the part I would not want to reconstruct by hand:

"search_space": {
  "alpha":  {"type": "real", "bounds": [0.0001, 100.0], "scale": "linear"},
  "solver": {"type": "categorical", "items": ["auto", "lsqr", "saga"]}
}

"evaluations": [
  {"trial": 0, "status": "complete", "score": -0.9437761069340015,
   "params": {"alpha": 94.29376099453265, "solver": "auto"}}, ...]

"surrogate": {
  "regressor": "sklearn.gaussian_process._gpr.GaussianProcessRegressor",
  "source": "default", "min_evals": 25, "fit_attempts": 5,
  "fit_failures": 0, "prediction_failures": 0, "local_optimization_failures": 0
}

"termination": {"termination_reason": "n_iter", "status": "completed"}

Two things to read carefully in that excerpt. score is stored in the search's minimization frame, so a 0.9438 accuracy appears as −0.9438. And surrogate.min_evals is the warm-up the run actually used — 25 here, the default — which is the number to compare against when a run reports that the surrogate never engaged, not the budget you thought you had.

When not to fit a surface

  • Small, flat spaces on a laptop. Three parameters, seconds per evaluation, accuracy differences of about a point at most. Use TPE or plain random search; the response surface cannot earn back its warm-up here, and the benchmark table says so in its own numbers.
  • Budgets below the warm-up. Under 25 evaluations, the default, nothing in the run is surrogate-guided at all: you pay the bookkeeping and get the sampling. Worth knowing before you interpret a null result.
  • When you cannot afford to trust the record. The surface is an approximation of what you have observed, not a certificate: it can be confidently wrong between samples, and no coverage guarantee is claimed for the search itself. It does not replace a held-out evaluation, and it does not make a flat objective steep.
  • When categorical features are encoded ordinally. The most actionable finding from the benchmark study was a defect in my own pipeline: encoding unordered categories as ordered integers gave the surface an adjacency structure the categories do not have, and it silently masked the surrogate's advantage. If your space has categorical parameters, look at the encoding before you look at the search.

Where it does pay: evaluations measured in minutes, a space with enough dimension that the warm-up is a small share of the budget, and a question about final quality rather than wall-clock. On the 1,220-pipeline space the search reached within 1 % of its own final best in a median of 6 evaluations, against 11–17 for every other arm at identical accuracy. It also lost that advantage on wall-clock — the Gaussian-process fit grows as O(n³), per-fit cost rose from 3 ms to 174 ms across a run, and 49 % of a 120 s budget went to search overhead. Both numbers are in the benchmark write-up, and they belong together.

Caveats I'm not hiding

The warm-up arithmetic is a property of this implementation, not of surrogate optimization in general; a different system with a different basis or an informed prior could start guiding earlier. The benchmark numbers come from seven seeds, budgets of 30 and 60 evaluations, and datasets small enough to run on a laptop, with a response surface that is un-tuned on purpose. The 60-evaluation extension covers the search arms only, so its comparison is against the other SRS variants rather than against TPE. The wall-clock arm uses a different currency and is not comparable to the evaluation-count tables. The warm-up count C(n + 3, n) is the term count of the polynomial surface the search was originally built around; once the default surface became a Gaussian process that number stopped being an exact requirement, and it is a guide to the order of magnitude rather than a theorem. The audit excerpt above comes from a real run on scikit-learn's bundled breast-cancer dataset with three-fold CV instead of the default five, so the score is not meaningful — the point is the shape of the record, not the number in it.

Every code path described here was executed against SKSurrogate 0.8.1 before publication, and the numbers quoted are the ones the code returned. If something in it does not match the version you have installed, that is a bug report worth sending.
